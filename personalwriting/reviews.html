  • A Study of Resonance

    • mymovingfingerwrites, 2023, 640,000 words
    Finished on 12th of September 2026
    ★★★⯪☆ ♥

    I'm not sure if I would normally review fanfic like this, but I enjoyed this one enough that it feels like it deserves a spot. It certainly had a lot to keep me going, considering its overall length is greater than all of the Lord of the Rings.

    In discussing the mythos of the Harry Potter franchise, a common refrain I've witnessed and used myself is that the structure of its world makes less sense the more you pry into it. It is very difficult, using all available textual evidence, to put together a framework for how things work or why. I'm caught between thinking that it's too great of a task because of a dearth of concrete information, and thinking it's impossible because various pieces of evidence we DO have are inherently contradictory.

    In general, I think fanfic opts to ignore canon in favor of a framework for magic that suits their ends. This fic is no different, adding entirely original ideas or at least expanding on them in ways that feel responsible and effective. Key example would be "magical portraiture." You ever wonder about how photographs are enchanted, and why some of them appear to be sentient while others are simple loops? This fic actually delves into that with some explicit detail.

    It doesn't escape from the overall pattern, however: in a conversation with McGonagall, reference is made to the four basic elements of ancient Greek philosophy, meant to serve as some sort of basis for why various magics work. I'm sure if the author attempted to expand on that too hard, it too would become contradictory, or at least become insufficient with more than light scrutiny.

    All of this is to say that this fic manages to juggle its time well enough between advancing a story while also being satisfying enough with its descriptions of how things work. I'm mostly talking about this in contrast with something like the infamous Yudkowsky fanfic Methods of Rationality. I hold Study of Resonance to be a superior version of that idea, where you have an intelligent protagonist using knowledge in creative ways to fix complex problems. Granted that Methods of Rationality is meant to be a didactic work (which it fails utterly at, in my opinion) and this one is more simply an exploration of what is kind of a ridiculous idea.

    To wit: the basis of this story is that Percy Weasley feels a horrible guilt at Fred's death, and those of other students from Hogwarts after the war with Voldemort. Using a ritual, he resolves to go back in time in possession of all the knowledge he has of how things unfold (with some cursory discussions with Harry Potter about what exactly was going on and why). He doesn't have the full picture, but he still has his prior schooling and all of his dealings at the Ministry from the first go around.

    This fic has what I think are some uncommon character interactions: Percy becomes very close with Moaning Myrtle, Snape, and various incarnations of Tom Riddle. I will not delve too deeply into it though; if these interest you, then by all means begin reading the story for yourself. And prepare for a little homoeroticism, if you're not used to that kind of thing.

    I was very impressed by this fic, although I think the author rushed in places or was simply pressed for time. It could have used another round of editing before publication of some chapters which are rife with typos, and at times the descriptions feel a bit mechanical. Overall though, I was pleasantly surprised by how much I enjoyed Percy's quest, and his explorations of the arcane. A high recommend for anyone who wants a good, lengthy Harry Potter fic.

  • Chasing Sunlight

    • Inadvisably Compelled, 2023, 166,000 words
    Finished on 14th of September 2026
    ★★⯪☆☆ ♥

    This is a shill featured on Makin's Shills List. I'm surprised by a couple of the reviews for this up on there, including Bolas, who insists that he hates this story and finds it totally ineffective. I agree that the prose and characters are a little formulaic: I can tell you the main objectives of each character very easily, but they lack deeper feeling of being people. Contrary to some of the criticism, I found the dialogue to be fine, or at least easy.

    On the other hand, sunlight as a metaphor for truth is like a surface level take of what this story is about. The themes of the story deal a lot more with PERSONAL truth, which I felt was handled well enough. At any given time the characters are grappling with their attachments to the world and how these shape them as people. I won't get into spoilers since the story is short enough that it can be read without much fuss, but suffice to say that I felt like the ending worked well enough.

    This being said, it does feel as if the encounters that the group of explorers undergo lack a kind of grit. Ukaresh is by far the best one, everything else feels like it touches too lightly on the world around them. I get the feeling that the author had the roadmap and names of places decided but did minimal planning for how the world interlocks outside of what the characters see. Still a fun enough romp if you can handle words that feel pretty obviously picked out of a thesaurus.

  • The Left Hand of Darkness

    • Ursula K. Le Guin, 1969, 85,000 words
    Finished on 27th of May 2026
    ★★★⯪☆ ♥

    The back half was definitely far better than the front, as it lacked the awkwardness of the first several chapters. I had a lot of thoughts during the main characters' trek across the ice sheet that I'll come back to.

    I'm amused by the comments that call the writing sexist, in a way. From a modern lens that is probably correct, though I can't help thinking about the time this was released and what more common perceptions of sex and gender would have been back in 1969. My own father was born in that year, and still to this day he mostly displays behavior that would be associated with traditional masculine norms, a loose machismo. During the course of discussion, Alice appropriately brought up that progressive ideological thought surrounding gender wasn't stagnant or necessarily primitive back then, but I can't help believing that the vast majority of people either did not know or did not care about such things, at least not in my geographical region. All of this is to say that I think that, while accurate, accusing the book of being sexist is rather unfair.

    In a way I think the book is very not sexist. There's a passage or two I found to be very compelling: I think it's important to remember that these characters (aside from Genly Ai) are fundamentally not male or female, they are specifically androgynous and commonly display features that we would associate with both sexes. Much of this book is about developing an appreciation for cultures literally alien to our own, and it is too easy to interpret what is (intended to be) completely different as being more similar to us than it really is. A few times at least, I think the story succeeds in highlighting the Gethenians' psychology. Genly is constantly rationalizing the behavior of various people he's met by comparing it to behaviors he associates as being masculine or feminine. This is also consistently rebuffed as a foundational misunderstanding of how Gethenians actually work.

    Outside of the sex/gender aspect of the book, I also had the realization that Dune had come out just four years prior to this book's publication, which most likely had a significant impact on the descriptions of ecology and the development/use of technology. It's not as robust as Frank Herbert's stuff by any means, but the emphasis of language to describe the different types of precipitation was fun, and I really liked the idea of the Chabe stove even if it seems unrealistically effective.

    In all, a worthwhile pick. I'll probably have to read Le Guin's other works in this vein.

  • The Man in the High Castle

    • Philip K. Dick, 1962, 70,000 words
    Finished on 5th of September 2026
    ★★★★☆ ♥

    After ruminating on this for a little while, I think this could be the best book I read this year unless I'm lucky. I was astonished by how much I liked it. The alternate history concept of Nazi Germany (and Imperial Japan) winning WWII is probably more popularized to people of my age and younger by the property Wolfenstein, but this rendering was--perhaps to no one's surprise--far more tasteful and nuanced. The surprise standout cast member was Mr. Tagomi, whose spiritual considerations outshined basically everything else by a large margin. The philosophical war he engages in with the piece of jewelry near the end of the book was nothing short of enthralling. No one in the cast was a bad addition to the story though, not even the mean, stunted Robert Childan.

    One could probably point out the story's treatment of the Japanese imperialists as a bit fetishistic by modern standards, but I'm not so sure. It is clear that both of the major factions in charge of the world have their problems, many of them severe. Instead of considering it on a wider scale, I think that the individuals present make this story in a big way. Their private considerations are moving, and their debate or pleas with the world around them hit hard. There's a quiet desperation present in almost every character whose perspective we see. I can't remember reading any of Philip K. Dick's other stories but I'm going to have to now.

    An unexpected effect of reading this is that I grew fascinated by everyone's usage of the I Ching, or the Book of Changes of ancient Chinese culture. Falling out of popularity after 1900 AD in real life, I wonder what exactly led to the author picking it up for this novel (he apparently consulted the oracle in writing the book which led to a somewhat unorthodox structure). I've dabbled in mysticism before but do not put any real stock in it, so I suspect it will be little more than a passing fancy for me. For the meantime, though, it has been somewhat enriching to read about this tradition that has all but passed away. Its use throughout this book is nothing short of inspired.

  • Tinker Tailor Soldier Spy

    • John le Carre, 1974, 100,000 words
    Finished on 17th of August 2026
    ★★★⯪☆ ♥

    The only spy novel I've ever read, and probably my only real exposure to traditional spy media (I'm unsure how much Mission: Impossible qualifies, and have somehow never seen a James Bond movie. There are two comedies I've seen, Get Smart (2008) with Steve Carell or Johnny English (2003) with Rowan Atkinson, but I certainly don't count them). I'm unsure what I expected, but this is very obviously British in tone and feeling. It has a rather understated feel to basically everything, with the climax of the story being little more than catching two people in the act of talking about treasonous espionage.

    This is to the story's benefit, in my opinion, allowing one to soak in the details of the plot without being overwhelmed by Things Happening. While I found it a bit overwhelming to get used to John le Carre's vernacular, I was rather taken in by at after a while and looked up a comprehensive glossary so I could keep up. The more nuanced twists and turns of why characters were doing certain things or what certain information meant was a refreshing change from the fantasy or sci-fi novels I'm more accustomed to reading, where either the impetus or the result or both tend to be rather obvious in their scope.

    The character of George Smiley was also rather interesting. Not immediately relatable, yet I felt compelled to respect him in some ways. One particular detail that stuck out to me was how his physical appearance contrasted with his character: specifically, he is described as rather obese (though given this was in Europe in the 1970s, that assessment might be contested now), suggesting that he lacks control. Throughout the story, however, Smiley is a figure of immense restraint and patience, never losing his temper throughout the course of the present conflict. It's a very minor detail in the scheme of things, but I found it remarkable for some reason.

    I would like to read more spy novels after this, especially those in the rest of Carre's oeuvre, but it's rather low on my list of things to do. I'm not really given to mystery stories in general, but maybe one of these will grip me more than I'm expecting.

  • The Trial

    • Franz Kafka, 1925, 48,000 words
    Finished on 27th of June 2026
    ★★★☆☆

    I read Metamorphosis back in college, of which I remember only the barest of bones. I thought I would thus be prepared for this, but I was taken aback by just how discomfiting this work is. Part of that is certainly diegetic, where the myriad problems of society Josef K is faced with confuse and provoke you constantly; I do think that it's clear why this novel was only published posthumously, though, seeing as it's literally unfinished in certain places. I'm sure some further editing from Kafka himself would have yielded some needed polish.

    This is not to say the book isn't really good in its current form. There is a lot to unpack in it: the seeming construction of society as a whole is jarring until you piece together that everyone is in everyone else's back pocket in some way. Much clamor is made about the law, but the law's exact form feels like vapor. Customs and rules are impenetrable to anyone not in the know. Josef himself is like an animal, ruled by his immediate passions and desires at times (going so far as to assault the young woman Bürstner). His conversations with the painter and at the church near the end are both illuminating and yet exhausting. A sense of dread looms over everything.

    We were having a discussion about this on the Homestuck Discord (this book being the Book Club entry at the time), and Alice made an interesting comment about how Josef feels like he is above the legal proceedings of the titular Trial, but in trying to call it out or resist the affair, he becomes a greater victim thereof. We never learn what the charges are, and instructions pertaining to the legal process are practically nonexistent. One might naturally consider this to be a farce, as Josef does, and he tries very hard to withdraw from the nightmare. In doing so he upsets the matter even further, and at the end of the story he is executed (although the manner in which this happens makes it feel extrajudicial, more like murder).

    I'm not really sure what to tease apart from all this. Josef is hardly blameless: he is the chief clerk of a bank and treats his subordinates in the same way he despises to be treated by his superiors, in and out of the banking system. His treatment of women is also obviously terrible. The end result is something of a morass. The only real thematic connection I can make is a suggestion that society is a frightening labyrinth, which feels a bit too pedestrian for this (I'm certainly forgetting historical context that Kafka was going through over the course of his lifetime).

    Not my cup of tea in the end. Regardless, worth the read for the challenge alone.

  • 2 Fast 2 Furious

    • John Singleton, 2003, 1 hour and 47 minutes
    Watched on 16th of February 2025
    ★★★⯪☆ ♥

    This movie was bizarre for somewhat minor reasons. I am surprised yet again: I thought Vin Diesel was in all of these, but he was not in this one (nor will he be in Tokyo Drift apparently, spoiled myself on the fact that one's a standalone story). Paul Walker was an effective protagonist for this sequel though.

    In keeping with the tones of homoeroticism (which were SUBSTANTIALLY heightened throughout), Brian has changed roles from the uke to the seme. His love interest is now Roman Pearce, played by Tyrese Gibson. I described the gay subtext in the first movie plenty: suffice it to say that a gender and sexuality studies major could effortlessly turn this into their capstone project.

    The plot is like a more threadbare version of the first one, so I will not bother to describe it beyond saying that I felt the minor cast was done a lot more justice in this--there's a bunch of people who you just never saw again in the first movie until they were suddenly needed again, but in 2F2F you see the like sidekick drivers a lot more consistently and they're much more fun (top of the list is Suki screaming "SMACK THAT ASS" as she flies through the air in her hot pink car).

    Far more fascinating to me were certain elements of the production: whenever someone used nitro, there would be this incredible warp speed effect on the visuals outside the car. It was pretty well rendered but felt a bit silly. In general though, the film quality felt weirdly cheap compared to the first movie? Upon reviewing some clips of both, I believe the issue is that the first one used shadows and colors much more creatively (examples: there's a scene where Vin Diesel enters the kitchen to tackle his lover from a side area, with a shot of him emerging from full darkness. There are also a few scenes taking place in clubs where everything is bathed in various reds and blues), the lighting in 2 Fast 2 Furious is a lot more flat and even so that almost everything feels illuminated in white pretty effectively. Made it feel a bit more like a TV show than a big budget movie. I swear the overall resolution was lower though, and there were a couple of scenes that felt remarkably like green screened footage because of the color contrast being weird as hell.

    In point of fact I actually went to look up the budgets because I was dead convinced this one was significantly reduced. Completely wrong: the first movie was 38 million dollars and 2F2F was 76 MILLION. Aside from one scene where there is a fucking trillion cars on screen, I have no idea where most of that money went. Maybe that was Paul Walker money--it seems Vin Diesel was offered $25 million to return and he turned that down. I don't know if Walker had the same kind of star appeal at that point. Maybe most of it was spent on random partying in Miami?

  • Conan the Barbarian

    • John Milius, 1982, 2 hours and 9 minutes
    Watched on 5th of April 2025
    ★★★⯪☆ ♥

    Feels weird that I had never seen this before, it was right up my alley in some respects. Many times throughout college and after I've wondered about pre-historical societies that might have popped up and died away with no firm record of their existence--one can do a great deal with just wooden implements, although it often deteriorates without leaving much physical evidence. Putting aside the anachronistic use of steel and other materials that they could not reasonably have known about (and obviously all of the sorcery shit) this is a wonderful nod to that era of humanity steeped heavily in mythology which spawned all of the names we are now familiar with as figures of legend.

    There are a couple of scenes in this that were genuinely cool. At one point Conan is on the verge of death (or already dead) and his companions seek the aid of a wizard living on the edge of the wastes. He does a lot of esoteric bullshit and then in the middle of the night, Conan is wrapped in spirits which wish to tear him away into the afterlife. His companions literally wrestle with these ghosts to save him; it's not a particularly complex scene but something about its construction gave me goosebumps. I think the magic of it all was just a light enough touch that when stuff came down to brass tacks, it was rather impactful.

    To be frank, I was also expecting a lot more sexual harassment. On the contrary, they make it a point that Conan treats women relatively nicely, wrapping a concubine up in his furs to protect her dignity (right up until he has sex with her in front of their captors). On the whole though, very little is made of his encounters with women aside from his love interest. I found that kind of surprising and it added a lot of warmth to their relationship.

    Reading the Wikipedia article of this movie revealed that there was a whole host of this kind of crap that came out at the time, which I loosely knew about, but now I feel a vague urge to watch more of this kind of sword-and-sorcery stuff, although most of it sounds like cheap crap. We shall see either way.

It is greatly amusing that there was such an uproar about this movie containing anything that diverges even slightly from historical reality, both given the fantastical nature of the story and the success it's enjoyed since release.

    In all, the only thing that took me out of the movie was a misunderstanding I had near the end. It has been joked about quite a lot because it does sound kind of bad even in the right context: one may have heard the hamfisted lines where Odysseus says "our age of bronze is collapsing," which is easily the worst line of the movie. Much ado is also made about "the people of the sea," which I was taking to refer to "the Sea Peoples" which are a real life historical theory about why exactly the Mycenaean civilization vanished. I was working off of an outdated model of this idea where the Sea Peoples are a collection of people literally from across the Mediterranean, such as from the north and east coasts of Africa, whom then came to raze the Greek peninsula and islands. I initially thought that the People from the Sea mentioned in the movie are an enigmatic threat posed by said invaders. I thus had a rather heated argument with some people on Discord about what exactly Odysseus was talking about when he said "we are the People from the Sea," where they insisted that Odysseus was referring to his own homecoming sowing fear among his people.

    More modern scholarship suggest it was a combination of foreign invaders, but also a lot of people in the Greek peninsula and islands themselves that turned to piracy in times of hardship. Apparently this simply caused enough hardship that the Mycenaean city-states could not withstand the pressure, and trade routes collapsed after enough pillaging made it infeasible to continue. In Nolan's Odyssey, this seems to be a more favorable interpretation of the aforementioned line about "we are the People from the Sea." It was not literally that Odysseus and his men, and the rest of Agamemnon's fleet, were the People from the Sea, causing havoc and spreading rumors of the end of the world. Rather, their abuse of Zeus's law precipitated their own destruction in the vein of Greek myth, where hubris begets one's own downfall. Agamemnon wins the war, but he and his soldiers all pay a price for it.

    My original, faulty interpretation of this was almost enough to ruin the movie for me, but with the passage of time I really don't know what I was up in arms about. The film is a staggering accomplishment either way. If only someone could make a decent Iliad, although this movie shows enough of it that it's probably not worth tackling anytime soon. What I would REALLY like is a production that's well-funded and produced like this one, but which really takes its time to render the Iliad and the Odyssey as faithfully to the original text as possible. I doubt there's much of a real audience for that, but I bet if it were done well it could be something else.
